# EVALUATION_DIMENSIONS_ADVOCACY.md
## A Framework for Capturing an Association Advocacy Writer's Voice

This document defines the analytical dimensions used to extract a writer's distinctive voice from a corpus of association and advocacy communications: member columns, government-relations updates, memos, press releases, blog posts, and action alerts. The writer is typically a government-relations (GR) or policy staffer writing on behalf of a professional or trade association, and the reader is usually a member of that association.

It keeps the same nine dimensions as the scientific framework (`core/EVALUATION_DIMENSIONS.md`), with what-to-look-for questions rewritten for this genre. The goal is the same: identify what makes *this writer's* prose recognizable as theirs, not what makes advocacy writing good in the abstract. Observations that would describe any competent association communicator ("explains legislation clearly," "thanks members") carry no signal and are discarded unless the writer does them in a specific, recurring way.

---

### How documents contribute

**All documents contribute evenly.** There is no weighting. A pattern is strong because it recurs across documents, not because any document is long. Token counts are informational only.

**Author treatment.** Every registered document is read as the author's own voice in full, including quotations attributed to association leaders or spokespeople inside press releases. In GR shops these quotes are usually drafted by the writer, so they are in voice. Record them as a distinct *spokesperson* sub-register (see D9) rather than discarding them.

**Evidence discipline: quotes in notes, never in the skill.** Every observation in a notes file must be anchored by a verbatim quote from a source document. Synthesis distills the quotes into described patterns and never copies corpus sentences into the skill, because a specimen sentence in a loaded skill becomes a template the model oversamples.

**Recurrence across short documents.** Advocacy pieces are often short (200 to 700 words). A single short piece rarely shows enough to establish a habit. Where the corpus is read in one batch, report how many documents show each pattern (for example "9 of 14"). A pattern seen in one document is an anecdote, not a habit.

---

## Document types

**Column** (`column`): a recurring, bylined piece in a member magazine or newsletter, often on a fixed cadence (monthly, bimonthly). Moderate scaffold. The column's own recurring structure (a standing title, a regular results section, a sign-off) is a *column convention*, not necessarily the writer's habit. Look for what persists when the topic changes.

**Memo** (`memo`): an internal or member-facing briefing, usually headed (To / From / Date / Re) and organized under subheads or bullets. High scaffold for layout, low scaffold for the reasoning inside each section.

**Press release** (`press_release`): headline, dateline, lead, attributed quote(s), boilerplate. The most genre-constrained type. Voice shows mostly in the lead's framing, in the quotes, and in what the writer chooses to emphasize.

**Blog post** (`blog_post`): web-published, often more conversational than a column. Low scaffold.

**Action alert** (`action_alert`): a call to members to contact officials, usually with a deadline and a link. Very short, imperative. High signal for how the writer motivates and instructs.

**Member email** (`member_email`): direct correspondence to members or member groups. Lowest scaffold.

**Other** (`other`): specify in the document notes.

### The scaffold spectrum

```
Press release → Memo → Column → Blog post → Action alert → Member email
most constrained                                         least constrained
```

Voice signal increases as scaffold decreases. Pieces written in the genres the skill will actually be used for are worth more than additional pieces of a genre already well represented.

## Dimension 1 — Syntactic Writing Style

**What it captures:** Sentence construction habits independent of content.

**What to look for:**
- Typical sentence length and how much it varies. Do long sentences carry policy detail while short ones carry the point?
- How the writer opens sentences: with the actor ("Congress passed"), with the member ("You may have heard"), with time ("Last month"), or with the writer ("I").
- Use of first person singular ("I") versus institutional "we", and when each appears.
- Questions posed to the reader, and whether the writer answers them immediately.
- Characteristic constructions: fronted subordinate clauses, paired clauses joined by "and", parenthetical asides, lists inside sentences.

**Failure mode:** Short sentences are a genre norm in member communications. Only record sentence-length habits that are more pronounced, or patterned differently, than the genre requires.

---

## Dimension 2 — Semantic Writing Style

**What it captures:** How the writer builds meaning: what they explain, what they assume the reader knows, and how they make policy concrete.

**What to look for:**
- How much legislative or regulatory process the writer explains (bill stages, rulemaking, state versus federal) versus assumes.
- How the writer translates policy into consequences for the reader's work: through workplace examples, patient or customer outcomes, career impact, or costs.
- Whether the writer explains their own decisions and reasoning to members (why a campaign was run a certain way, why a position was taken).
- Use of analogy, story, or named examples versus abstract statement.
- How acronyms, bill numbers, and agency names are introduced and then referred to.

**Failure mode:** Explaining "what this means for you" is a standard advocacy move. Record *how* this writer makes the translation, not merely that they make it.

---

## Dimension 3 — Vocabulary and Lexical Choices

**What it captures:** Word choice that marks the writer.

**What to look for:**
- How the writer names the members and the profession (collective nouns, job titles, "you", "our members", "the profession").
- How the writer names officials and institutions ("elected officials", "lawmakers", "legislators", "your representative").
- Recurring verbs for advocacy actions (educate, advocate, engage, reach out, push, fight).
- Register of word choice: plain, warm, formal, technical, insider policy jargon.
- Intensifiers, hedges, and evaluative adjectives the writer reaches for, and their frequency.
- Words or phrasings the writer clearly avoids where peers would use them.

**Failure mode:** Organizational boilerplate and mission-statement language are institutional, not personal. Discount phrases that look copied from association branding.

---

## Dimension 4 — Rhythm and Cadence

**What it captures:** Paragraph-level pacing and the shape of a piece.

**What to look for:**
- Typical paragraph length, and whether one-sentence paragraphs are used for emphasis.
- How a piece opens: thanks, news, a question, context, a result.
- How a piece closes: thanks, a call to action, a forward look, a contact invitation, a personal note.
- The ratio of narrative prose to lists, tables, or headed sections.
- Repetition used for cadence (a repeated phrase or a returning thank-you).

**Failure mode:** Layout imposed by a magazine template or email platform (fixed headers, standard sign-offs) is not rhythm. Record only what varies with the writer's choices.

---

## Dimension 5 — Stance and Certainty

**What it captures:** How the writer positions themselves and the association relative to uncertain political outcomes, and how much authority they claim.

**What to look for:**
- How confident the writer sounds about legislation passing, rules being adopted, or campaigns succeeding. Do they predict, hope, or decline to predict?
- How they handle setbacks and slow progress: candor, reframing, silence.
- Whether opposing parties or officials are characterized, and how sharply.
- Partisan neutrality: how the writer stays (or does not stay) nonpartisan.
- How they credit members, partners, and staff versus claiming credit.
- Expressions of personal feeling or hope ("It is my hope") and how often.

**Failure mode:** Nonpartisan caution is often an association policy. Record the writer's characteristic way of being careful, not caution itself.

---

## Dimension 6 — Argumentation Structure

**What it captures:** How a piece moves from news to meaning to action.

**What to look for:**
- The order in which a piece presents what happened, why it matters, what the association did, and what members should do.
- How calls to action are framed: as a request, an instruction, an invitation, or an obligation; with or without a deadline; with or without reassurance that it is easy.
- How the writer anticipates and answers member questions or objections ("I received a couple of questions about...").
- How results are reported back to members after a campaign, and whether the writer evaluates them.
- Whether the writer argues from member benefit, public benefit (patients, safety, customers), professional recognition, or fairness.

**Failure mode:** Every action alert ends with a request. Record the writer's characteristic framing, placement, and justification of the request.

---

## Dimension 7 — Transition Logic

**What it captures:** How the writer connects ideas and sections.

**What to look for:**
- Connectives the writer relies on between paragraphs (time markers, "because", "so", "however", "in addition") and which they avoid.
- How topic shifts are handled in multi-item updates: subheads, bolded lead-ins, "Also", or no marker.
- How the writer moves from a result to its explanation, and from an explanation to the next step.
- Callbacks to earlier pieces or earlier campaigns ("as I mentioned in my last column").

**Failure mode:** Subheads in a memo template are layout, not logic. Look at transitions inside sections.

---

## Dimension 8 — Numbers and Specifics

**What it captures:** How the writer handles concrete particulars: figures, dates, bill numbers, officials, states, deadlines, and campaign metrics.

**What to look for:**
- How campaign or survey results are reported: tables, inline figures, rounded or exact numbers, year-over-year comparison.
- Whether numbers are interpreted for the reader immediately, and how (cause, comparison, caveat).
- How bill numbers, statute names, agency names, and officials' names and titles are cited, and how much detail is given.
- Dates and deadlines: precision and placement.
- Whether the writer names specific states, lawmakers, or partner organizations, or stays general.

**Failure mode:** Including bill numbers is a genre norm. Record the writer's habit of *framing* specifics, such as explaining a number's caveat or comparing to last year.

---

## Dimension 9 — Register Modulation

**What it captures:** How the voice shifts between genres (column, memo, press release, blog post, action alert, email) and between sub-registers inside a piece (narrator versus spokesperson quote, report versus appeal).

**What to look for:**
- What survives across every genre in the corpus: the sentence-level habits that are this writer's floor.
- How the attributed-quote (spokesperson) register differs from the narrator's register in press releases.
- How the writer shifts between reporting (results, status) and appealing (thanks, calls to action) inside one piece.
- In a single-genre corpus: what the genre seems to allow or suppress, as raw material for carrying the voice into other genres.

**Failure mode:** Differences that merely follow genre convention (a press release has a dateline) tell you nothing about the writer. The signal is how the writer handles a required shift.

---

## Notes on synthesis

- Order the skill by signal strength across documents, not by dimension number.
- With a single-genre corpus, the skill states plainly that it learned from that genre, carries the sentence-level voice into other genres, and follows each genre's standard conventions for layout. It does not invent a register the corpus does not show.
- Keep column conventions (standing titles, recurring results sections, fixed sign-offs) out of the voice description unless they carry over to other genres.
